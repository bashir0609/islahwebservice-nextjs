## Challenge

A cloud migration consultancy needed a prospect database built around technology-stack evidence rather than a broad list of companies in a selected industry. Its target accounts were organizations showing the legacy-infrastructure signals covered by the consultancy’s migration work. Company size and industry also formed part of the requested scope, so a technology mention by itself was not enough to establish a match.

This created a research challenge with several layers. The company first had to fit the consultancy’s size and industry requirements. Public sources then had to show the requested legacy-technology signal. Finally, the account needed a relevant technology or infrastructure contact, such as a CTO, VP of IT or infrastructure, or IT director.

The purpose of the work was to document observable matches against those client-defined conditions. A legacy technology signal could support inclusion in the research database, but it did not prove that a company intended to migrate, had an approved project, possessed budget, or wanted to engage a consultancy. Those commercial conclusions remained outside the evidence available for this project.

## Criteria

The research specification combined account-level and contact-level fields. At account level, each company needed to match the requested geography, industry, and size boundaries supplied for the project. It also needed evidence of one or more legacy-infrastructure technologies named in the brief. The original project source identifies the market as the UK and describes the target as companies running infrastructure that the consultancy’s migrations replace.

Technology evidence was treated as a research signal, not as a statement of purchase intent. The database therefore needed to retain the signal itself and the date on which it was checked. This gave the consultancy a way to distinguish a documented technology observation from a generic assumption based on company type.

At contact level, the requested roles included the CTO, VP of IT or infrastructure, and IT director. The appropriate title could vary by account, so role relevance depended on the organization’s published leadership structure. The task was to identify a current person in an applicable technology leadership function, not to claim that the person was the sole buyer or had final authority over a migration decision.

## Method

Research began with companies that appeared to fit the requested industry and size criteria. The next stage looked for public evidence of the specified legacy technologies. Rather than relying on a single isolated mention, the process cross-checked signals across multiple public sources where those sources were available.

The evidence collected for an account was translated into structured custom columns. Those columns captured the observed technology signal and its verification date. Keeping that context alongside the company record made the basis for inclusion reviewable and reduced the risk of turning a research observation into an unsupported commercial inference.

Once an account matched the stated criteria, role research focused on the CTO, VP of IT or infrastructure, and IT director. Public professional and company information was used to establish whether the named person currently held a relevant position. Contacts were then enriched and verified where the required business details were available.

The workflow separated discovery, evidence review, role identification, enrichment, and final formatting. That separation mattered because a correct company match did not automatically guarantee a current contact, and a relevant contact did not replace the need for account-level technology evidence. Records could therefore be reviewed field by field rather than accepted simply because one part of the profile looked plausible.

## QA

Quality assurance centered on whether each record could be traced back to the project criteria. Company records were checked for the requested size and industry fit, while technology-stack entries were reviewed against more than one public source where possible. The recorded verification date helped communicate when the technology evidence had last been assessed.

Contact QA focused on role currency and relevance. A title had to align with the requested technology, infrastructure, or IT leadership functions. Where organizations used different title conventions, the function mattered more than forcing every account into an identical label. Contact details were verified where available, while unavailable details were not to be represented as confirmed.

The final dataset was also deduplicated and standardized. This included reviewing repeated companies or contacts, normalizing comparable fields, and checking that custom technology columns remained attached to the correct account. The source states that contacts were enriched and verified and that every record carried technology signals and a verification date; a publishable quantitative validation rate or field-level accuracy result would require evidence: **METRIC-NEEDED**.

QA did not attempt to certify migration readiness, budget, urgency, or intent. Those factors were not established by the public technology signals used for this research.

## Deliverable

The consultancy received a CRM-ready prospect database containing criteria-matched companies, relevant technology leadership contacts, and the supporting technology-stack context. Custom columns preserved the legacy-technology signals and verification dates so the consultancy could understand why each account had been included.

The records were enriched, verified where possible, deduplicated, and standardized for practical import and review. Relevant contacts covered CTO, VP of IT or infrastructure, and IT director functions according to what could be identified at each organization. The database was the completed research product; it did not include managed outreach, meeting booking, sales execution, or migration delivery.

## Outcome Type

This engagement produced an evidence-led prospect research asset for the consultancy’s own business-development workflow. Its value was structural: the database connected each company to client-defined firmographic criteria, observable legacy-technology signals, relevant IT leadership, and a dated basis for review.

The outcome should therefore be described as a criteria-matched, CRM-ready research deliverable rather than a revenue, pipeline, meeting, or migration outcome.
