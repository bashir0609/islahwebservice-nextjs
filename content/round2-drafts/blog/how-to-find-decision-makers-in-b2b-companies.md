## How to Find Decision Makers in B2B Companies

“Find me the decision maker” is one of the most common requests in B2B prospecting — and one of the least precise. A decision maker is not a universal title. It is a person with a function, level of responsibility, and place within a specific company’s structure. The research job is to identify people holding roles relevant to the client’s offer, confirm that those roles appear current, and deliver sourced contact data for the client’s outreach.

This guide explains how that research works: translating a target role into searchable terms, understanding company structure, checking multiple sources, enriching the record, and being honest about what public data cannot confirm.

## Translate “Decision Maker” Into a Role Map

The phrase becomes researchable only when it is broken into criteria:

- **Function** — sales, marketing, HR, finance, IT, security, operations, procurement, or another department
- **Seniority** — C-level, vice president, director, head, manager, or owner
- **Company context** — size, industry, business model, geography, and organizational stage
- **Offer context** — the problem area your product or service addresses
- **Coverage rule** — one primary contact, several stakeholders, or a defined fallback hierarchy

“Decision maker for a recruitment agency selling to technology companies” might become “Talent Acquisition Managers, Heads of Talent, and HR Directors at Series B or later technology companies in Germany.” The funding stage is a client-defined company filter. The titles are role hypotheses to verify, not proof of authority.

A role map should include title variants because organizations use different language. “Chief People Officer,” “People Director,” “VP People,” and “Head of People” may overlap, but their responsibilities are not automatically identical. Record the actual title rather than rewriting it to fit a standard label.

## Research the Company Before the Person

Titles only make sense in relation to company structure. Before selecting a contact, check:

- **Company size and stage** — a Head of Sales in a small startup may cover operations that a larger company divides across several teams
- **Business model** — a software vendor, consultancy, manufacturer, and property business organize functions differently
- **Regional structure** — global, regional, and country-level leadership may coexist
- **Parent and subsidiary relationships** — the relevant person may work for a trading brand, operating subsidiary, or parent company
- **Recent changes** — acquisitions, leadership appointments, restructuring, and office openings can affect role ownership
- **Centralized versus local purchasing** — public structure may suggest possible stakeholders but does not prove the approval process

Start with a clean company record: canonical name, website, headquarters, industry, size range, and relationship to any parent or subsidiary. This prevents a correct person from being attached to the wrong account.

## Build a Buying-Committee Hypothesis, Not a Fiction

Many B2B purchases involve several stakeholders. Research can identify likely role families based on the offer, but it should not claim to know the internal committee without evidence.

A simple role framework is:

1. **Functional owner:** responsible for the business area affected by the offer.
2. **Technical evaluator:** assesses implementation, integration, security, or architecture where relevant.
3. **Operational user:** understands day-to-day workflow and requirements.
4. **Procurement or legal stakeholder:** may review terms, suppliers, or risk at larger organizations.
5. **Executive sponsor:** may support strategic initiatives or larger purchases.

Not every company has all five. A small business may combine them in one founder; a global company may distribute them across regions. Treat the framework as a search plan, then report only what the evidence supports.

## Sources for Finding People

### Company websites

Leadership, team, governance, newsroom, and contact pages are strong first-party sources. Press releases can confirm appointments and provide exact titles. Check the date: an announcement proves that the appointment occurred, not that the person remains in the role indefinitely.

### Professional profiles

LinkedIn and similar professional profiles can provide current titles, dates, and role descriptions. Use the visible profile and company context rather than relying only on a search-engine snippet. LinkedIn’s [public profile visibility guidance](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a522735) explains that the information visible publicly depends on member settings, so lack of a public result does not prove that no profile exists.

### Regulatory filings and registries

Official sources are useful for statutory officers and regulated entities. In the United States, [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) provides company filings that can name executives and directors. In the United Kingdom, [Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/) provides company and officer information. These sources do not identify every operational decision maker, and an officer is not automatically the relevant buyer for a specific service.

### Press coverage and conference materials

Credible interviews, speaker biographies, and event agendas can help confirm expertise and current responsibilities. Prefer the event organizer or employer page, record publication dates, and avoid treating an old biography as current without another check.

### Job postings

A vacancy may reveal department structure, reporting lines, or named leadership. It can also indicate that the target role is vacant. If the company is hiring a Head of Security, do not assign a former or interim title-holder without current evidence.

## A Step-by-Step Decision-Maker Research Workflow

### Step 1: Define the role hierarchy

Agree on primary, secondary, and fallback role families. Add excluded titles to prevent broad searches from capturing consultants, assistants, recruiters, or unrelated regional roles.

Example:

- Primary: VP Revenue Operations, Head of Revenue Operations
- Secondary: Sales Operations Director, Commercial Operations Director
- Fallback: COO at companies below the client-defined size threshold
- Exclude: Revenue Operations Consultant, former employees, fractional advisers unless requested

### Step 2: Validate the account

Confirm that the company matches the project criteria before researching people. Check domain, location, industry, size, and operating status. Resolve duplicate brands and subsidiaries.

### Step 3: Search first-party sources

Review leadership pages, newsroom posts, annual reports, governance pages, and team directories. Capture the title exactly as published and the source date.

### Step 4: Cross-check current employment

Compare first-party evidence with a current professional profile or another credible source. Look for conflicting start and end dates, recent promotions, or a profile showing a different employer.

### Step 5: Apply the hierarchy

Select contacts according to the approved role map, not intuition. If no primary role exists, use the documented fallback. If several people match, include them only if the brief permits multiple contacts.

### Step 6: Enrich the contact

Add business email where available, professional profile URL, location, company fields, and requested custom fields. Keep discovery status separate from verification status. A plausible email pattern is not the same as a verified mailbox.

### Step 7: Record evidence and uncertainty

Useful fields include person-source URL, company-source URL, role-verified date, email status, title confidence, and research notes. If sources disagree, flag the record for review instead of choosing the most convenient result.

### Step 8: Run quality assurance

Check for former employees, duplicate contacts, wrong subsidiaries, generic inboxes, title normalization errors, unsupported authority labels, and stale sources. Sample records should be traced back to their evidence before delivery.

## Example: Finding Contacts for an MSP Offer

Suppose an IT service provider wants mid-sized professional-services firms in a defined region. The service relates to cloud infrastructure and security.

The role map might prioritize IT Directors, Heads of Infrastructure, and technology leaders, with an operations leader as a fallback only for smaller companies. Researchers would first validate each firm against the company criteria, then review first-party leadership pages and current professional profiles. They might add a security or procurement stakeholder when the brief explicitly calls for multiple contacts.

The final record could state that a person is the current IT Director according to two dated sources. It should not state that this person owns the budget, wants to switch providers, or has approved a project unless a source explicitly supports that claim.

## Enrichment: What Gets Delivered

For each identified person, a useful record may include:

- Full name
- Exact current title
- Standardized function and seniority fields
- Company and company domain
- Location where relevant
- Professional profile URL
- Business email and verification classification
- Person-source URL and role-verification date
- Company criteria and source URLs
- Notes on conflicts, fallbacks, or uncertainty

Enrichment fills missing fields; verification checks them. Email validation assesses the address, role checks assess current employment, and company checks confirm that the account matches the brief. These are separate checks and should remain separate in the data.

## What Public Research Cannot Confirm

Public data may show that someone holds a role. It usually cannot confirm that the person:

- Has final budget authority
- Is the sole decision maker
- Is actively evaluating vendors
- Is dissatisfied with an incumbent provider
- Has approved timing or budget
- Will respond to outreach

Public evidence can sometimes reveal a formal responsibility — for example, a company biography may say an executive leads global procurement. Even then, it does not establish that the person controls a specific purchase.

Anyone guaranteeing authority, intent, replies, or meetings is making a different claim from prospect research. A verified current role at a criteria-matched company is a strong starting point, not a promise of an outcome.

## Keep the Data Current

Roles change, companies restructure, and websites lag behind reality. There is no universal refresh interval that makes every record current. Set freshness rules based on the campaign, market, source quality, and cost of an incorrect record.

Before use, prioritize rechecking:

- Contacts whose source is older than the approved window
- Senior appointments after mergers or funding events
- People with conflicting employer information
- Generic or pattern-generated emails
- Accounts with renamed domains or subsidiaries
- Records marked “interim,” “acting,” or “fractional”

The client should receive the date each material field was checked, not an unsupported promise that the database will remain accurate indefinitely.

## Responsible Outreach Use

Research and outreach are distinct stages. Data-protection and direct-marketing requirements depend on jurisdiction, data source, channel, purpose, and business type. For UK organizations, the Information Commissioner’s Office publishes guidance on [business-to-business marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/). Teams should assess the rules applying to their own processing and campaigns.

## Building a Decision-Maker List

A decision-maker list is only as useful as its criteria, evidence, and verification dates. Define the role map, validate companies first, cross-check current employment, enrich carefully, and retain uncertainty instead of disguising it.
