## How to Research Companies by Technology Stack

If you sell a CRM migration, a cybersecurity product, or a cloud tool, one of the most useful filters you can apply to a prospect list is “companies that use the technology we replace or complement.” Technology signals are public and checkable when the relevant software leaves a visible footprint. Unlike inferred intent, they describe evidence of what a company appears to run, not what someone speculates the company wants.

This guide explains which technology signals are researchable, where they come from, how to validate them, how to combine them with other criteria, and where the limits are. The goal is not to label every company with a perfect stack. It is to build a transparent, criteria-matched list in which every important technology field has a source and verification date.

## Which Technology Signals Are Researchable

The most useful technology indicators for B2B prospecting include:

- **CRM and sales tools** — Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics, marketing automation, and sales-engagement platforms
- **Cloud and infrastructure** — AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, content-delivery networks, hosting providers, and visible database technologies
- **Cybersecurity indicators** — email-security, certificate, DNS, endpoint, and perimeter vendors where a public footprint exists
- **Ecommerce and payments** — storefront platforms, payment gateways, tag managers, and cart technologies
- **Product and development stack** — frameworks, hosting, analytics, support widgets, and libraries exposed in public pages
- **Email and productivity** — Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 indicators, which can matter for integrations and migration services

Some products are easier to detect than others. A storefront script, analytics tag, DNS record, or response header can be visible from outside the company. An internal finance platform or self-hosted application may leave no public trace. Absence of evidence should therefore be recorded as “not detected,” not “does not use.”

Technology-detection services explain this distinction in their own documentation. [Wappalyzer’s documentation](https://www.wappalyzer.com/docs/technology-detection/) describes detection through website signals, while [BuiltWith](https://builtwith.com/) provides technology profiles based on observable web usage. These tools are useful discovery sources, but neither should be treated as an infallible inventory of a company’s internal systems.

## Start With a Researchable Brief

“Find Salesforce users” is too broad. Before searching, translate the commercial request into fields that a researcher can apply consistently:

1. **Target technology:** exact product, product family, or category.
2. **Relationship to your offer:** replacement, integration, implementation, compliance, or support.
3. **Company criteria:** industry, geography, employee range, ownership type, and operating status.
4. **Evidence threshold:** one detection source, two independent sources, or a first-party confirmation.
5. **Freshness window:** how recent the observation must be.
6. **Exclusions:** agencies, subsidiaries, existing customers, competitors, or unsupported regions.
7. **Required contacts:** role families and seniority levels to research after company validation.

For example, a useful brief might be: “UK software companies with 50–500 employees, currently showing HubSpot on a public web property, excluding consultancies, with one revenue-operations or marketing-operations contact.” The employee range is a client-defined filter, not a claim that every company in that range needs a migration.

## Where Technology Evidence Comes From

A defensible record usually combines several source types.

### Website and browser evidence

Page source, JavaScript bundles, cookies, network requests, response headers, and HTML attributes can reveal analytics, CMS, ecommerce, chat, and marketing tools. Browser developer tools can help verify whether a detected script is loaded on the current site rather than merely mentioned in old code.

### DNS and email infrastructure

Public DNS records can indicate email providers, domain-verification records, security services, and hosting infrastructure. [ICANN’s registration data lookup](https://lookup.icann.org/en) can help confirm domain context, while DNS records should be checked directly with an appropriate lookup tool. Registration data does not prove technology usage, but it can help resolve whether the domain belongs to the intended company.

### Job postings

A company recruiting a “Salesforce Administrator,” “Azure Platform Engineer,” or “Shopify Developer” provides first-party evidence that the technology is relevant to a role. However, a job description may describe a planned migration, a customer environment, or a preferred skill rather than the company’s current stack. Capture the wording and URL rather than reducing it immediately to a yes/no field.

### Company and vendor materials

Case studies, partner directories, implementation announcements, documentation, and customer stories can offer stronger evidence than passive detection. Check the publication date and whether the named company is the same legal entity or brand you are researching.

### Technical repositories and documentation

Public engineering blogs, status pages, package files, and repositories may identify frameworks or infrastructure. Use care: a public repository can be an experiment or an abandoned project rather than the production stack.

## A Step-by-Step Tech-Stack Research Workflow

### Step 1: Build a broad company universe

Start with companies matching non-technology criteria such as geography, industry, and size. This prevents a technology database from defining your market for you. Record the canonical company name, website, headquarters, and source.

### Step 2: Run technology discovery

Search one or more detection databases and record the product, category, source URL, and observed date. Keep raw observations separate from your final classification. If one provider says Salesforce and another has no result, that is not necessarily a contradiction.

### Step 3: Check first-party evidence

Review the company’s website, careers page, technical documentation, partner pages, and recent job listings. Search for exact product names and common variants. A current job advertisement or implementation announcement may provide useful context that a detection result cannot.

### Step 4: Classify the confidence level

Use a simple, transparent scheme:

- **Confirmed:** recent first-party statement or multiple current technical signals
- **Probable:** one credible current detection with supporting context
- **Historical:** valid evidence exists, but it is outside the project’s freshness window
- **Not detected:** no usable evidence found
- **Conflicting:** sources point to different products or periods

Do not silently convert “not detected” into “does not use.”

### Step 5: Apply the remaining filters

Validate size, industry, geography, and operating status. Remove duplicate domains and parent/subsidiary overlaps according to the brief. A perfect technology match is still irrelevant if the company fails a required criterion.

### Step 6: Research relevant people

Map the offer to a role family. A CRM implementation may involve revenue operations, sales operations, IT, or business systems. A cloud-security integration may involve infrastructure, security, or engineering leadership. Public research can confirm a person’s current role; it cannot prove that the person has budget authority or is evaluating vendors.

### Step 7: Deliver evidence with the record

Useful columns include company, domain, detected technology, technology category, evidence URL, evidence text, observation date, confidence, company criteria, contact name, current title, LinkedIn URL, business email status, and notes. A separate “conflict” or “manual review” field keeps uncertainty visible.

## Combining Technology With Other Criteria

A technology signal on its own is often a weak filter. Technology research earns its place when combined with:

- **Company size** — deployment complexity can differ substantially by organization size
- **Geography** — procurement, support coverage, and data-residency requirements vary by region
- **Industry** — the same platform may serve different processes in fintech, retail, and professional services
- **Hiring signals** — open roles can show where a company is investing, without proving purchase intent
- **Funding or expansion** — these provide timing context, not evidence that a vendor review is underway
- **Contract or migration language** — public roles mentioning implementation, consolidation, or migration may justify closer review

## Practical Examples

### Replacement research

A CRM consultancy wants companies that appear to use a competing CRM. The researcher identifies the target product, applies geography and size filters, checks current detection dates, and looks for first-party references. The final list says “observed using Product A” with evidence. It does not say “unhappy with Product A” or “ready to migrate.”

### Integration research

A software company integrates with a specific ecommerce platform and accounting tool. Researchers look for companies where both technologies are visible. Dual confirmation produces a narrower list, but the integration combination is more relevant than either technology alone.

### Technical services research

A managed service provider wants organizations using Microsoft 365 and operating in a defined region. DNS signals can help identify the productivity environment, while company checks confirm location and sector. The [MSP and IT services research page](/industries/msp) provides more context for building this type of criteria-matched database.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Treating a single vendor result as permanent truth
- Using a detection timestamp that falls outside the agreed freshness window
- Assuming a product mention proves production use
- Confusing a subsidiary’s stack with the parent company’s stack
- Recording “not detected” as a negative fact
- Claiming technology usage proves dissatisfaction, budget, or purchase intent
- Omitting evidence URLs and verification dates
- Researching contacts before validating the company

Technology environments change, and public visibility is incomplete. AWS’s guidance on [documenting workloads and architecture](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/document-your-architecture.html) illustrates why organizations themselves need current architectural records; an external researcher has access to a much narrower public view.
