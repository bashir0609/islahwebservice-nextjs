## How to Define Prospect Research Criteria (Before You Build a List)

Most prospect lists are bad before a single record is gathered — because the brief was bad. "Build me a list of decision makers in our industry" produces exactly what it deserves: a generic list that matches the *category* but not the *customer*. The difference between a useful list and a useless one is decided in the research brief, not the research.

This guide covers how to define prospect research criteria: company filters, signal filters, decision-maker roles, and required fields — the four parts of a brief a researcher can actually execute.

## Part 1: Company Criteria

Company criteria define *which organizations* are in scope. Each one should be a checkable filter:

- **Industry** — precise verticals and sub-verticals, not broad categories
- **Geography** — country, region, city, or radius
- **Size** — employee count or revenue bands
- **Structure** — ownership, subsidiaries, or business model where relevant
- **Stage** — funding stage, age, or maturity where relevant

Write each criterion so a researcher can check it against a source. "Companies we'd like to work with" is not a criterion; "manufacturers with 50–500 employees in the UK" is.

## Part 2: Signal Criteria

Signal criteria are the indicators that separate a priority account from a merely eligible one. Useful signals are public and verifiable:

- **Funding** — stage and date windows
- **Hiring** — open roles, hiring velocity, specific departments
- **Growth** — headcount change, expansion, new locations
- **Technology** — the tools or platforms a company runs
- **Events** — leadership changes, acquisitions, product launches

Signals are filters, not judgments. A company that matches your funding window and hiring criteria is a *criteria-matched account* — it shows the indicators you requested. It is not a company proven to be evaluating vendors or ready to buy.

## Part 3: Decision-Maker Roles

Decision-maker criteria define *which people* to find at each matched company. The translation is the same as the company side: roles, not vibes.

- **Function** — sales, marketing, HR, finance, IT, operations
- **Seniority** — C-level, VP, director, manager, head of
- **Relationship to your offer** — the functions your product or service touches

The research identifies who currently holds those roles and verifies the title is current. What research cannot confirm is budget authority or sole-decision status — the brief should not promise it.

## Part 4: Required Fields

Finally, define the fields every record must include. Standard prospect fields:

- Company: name, website, industry, size, location
- Contact: full name, current title, business email, LinkedIn URL
- Verification: verification date, and any checks applied
- Custom fields: anything your workflow depends on

Required fields drive both enrichment and quality control. If a field is required, the researcher reports it as filled, missing, or unverifiable — never guesses.

## The Brief as a Conversation

The best research briefs are written in conversation with the researcher, not thrown over the wall. A good researcher pushes back on:

- Criteria that are too broad to be useful
- Signals that aren't publicly verifiable
- Roles that don't map to real titles
- Fields that can't be reliably filled

The output is a brief both sides can execute: criteria that can be checked, signals that can be found, roles that can be mapped, and fields that can be delivered.
